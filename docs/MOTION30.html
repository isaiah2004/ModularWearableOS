Smooth at 30

How to make motion on a 30 fps watch read as fluid, intentional and fast, and how the NOS engine and apps apply it. Built for the Waveshare 1.43-B: a 466 × 466 round AMOLED, about 36 mm across, fed over 40 MHz QSPI with no tearing-effect line.
2026-10-12 · branch nothing-os · demos replay NOS's real curves and springs (ported from nos/motion.c) at exactly 30 fps
Measuring your screen…

We can't reach 60 fps on this board. A full repaint costs about 27 ms of work, and the bus alone takes 23.5 ms. NOS therefore runs at a flawless 30. But "30 fps" is a budget, not a look: the same 30 frames can feel like a premium device or a slideshow. Smoothness at low frame rates comes from what each frame shows, not how many frames there are. These are the four things that make 30 fps look bad, and each has a fix:

  • Uneven pacing. A single 66 ms frame in a run of 33 ms frames is more visible than a steady lower rate.
  • Big steps per frame. Once an object jumps further than the eye's short-range motion system can bridge, motion turns into strobing copies.
  • Hold blur. AMOLED holds every frame for the full 33 ms, so a tracking eye smears a moving object by its per-frame step.
  • Latency. A finger-driven object that trails the finger feels heavy. At 30 fps, every pipeline stage costs twice as much as at 60.

On this board there's a fifth: tearing, because the panel's TE pin isn't routed.

00The rules on one page

R1Even cadence beats speed. Fixed 30 Hz deadlines, time-based motion that jumps ahead after a late frame, clamped stalls, and no first-frame jank inside the animation.
R2Respect the step budget. ≤ 16 px per frame for anything the eye tracks. Up to ~64 px in short transitions. Bigger only as a deliberate single-frame cut.
R3Author spacing for frames. Count durations in whole frames. Choose curves by their worst frame: peak step ≤ 1.6× the average (EASE_STEADY). No first frames near 0 %.
R4Move less, fade more. Programmatic transitions travel ≤ 60 px and let ink fades carry the change, or they cut: anticipate, jump in one frame, settle 4-2-1-0.
R5Crisp endings. Snap within 1 px or 1 dot. A spring never creeps for 6 frames of 0.2–1.5 px full repaints.
R6Honest sub-pixels. AA shapes move at float positions. Text and sprites round the cumulative position, and slow scrolls use whole-pixel speeds.
R7Smear what is fast. Small objects moving > 16 px/frame get one or two ghosts (≈45 % / 20 % ink, ≤ 2 frames) or a velocity stretch. Cost: one primitive.
R8Quantise on purpose. Dot morphs, split-flap steps and decorative loops on twos read as style. Never step something the eye tracks.
R9Whole-frame rhythm. Staggers of 1–2 frames, ≤ 8 items. Fades in 16 gamma-spaced ink levels. Durations from the frame table, not milliseconds.
R10Cut latency, then predict. Sample touch at 60–100 Hz with timestamps and use the newest sample just before tick. Extrapolate drags by ≤ ½ frame. Least-squares fling velocity.
R11Design around tearing. Prefer vertical motion and small per-frame deltas over full-screen fast horizontal slides. Keep writes in row order.
R12Keep frame cost flat. Motion frames under 80 % of budget. Prebake and keep damage small, so the cadence never breaks mid-gesture.

01Why 30 fps can look smooth

Film runs at 24 fps and nobody calls it choppy. That works because of three things a UI has to do on purpose:

  • The frames are perfectly even.
  • Each frame carries motion blur. The 180° shutter exposes for half the frame time.
  • Camera moves are kept slow. Cinematographers' pan-speed rules exist because fast pans on 24 fps judder.

A watch UI has no shutter and moves things far and fast. So we have to supply evenness, keep steps small, add blur ourselves where it helps, and make large changes in ways the eye doesn't try to track.

02Even cadence beats speed

R1. Present on a fixed 30 Hz grid.
  • Advance animation by real elapsed time, quantised to whole frames, so motion stays on schedule after a late frame.
  • Clamp long stalls.
  • Start an animation's clock on its first presented frame.
  • Targets: frame-time jitter under 4–5 ms, at most one missed slot per ~7 s of motion (hitch ratio < 5 ms/s), p95 frame interval ≤ 40 ms.

The perception evidence is consistent:

  • In a CHI 2023 study, the 95th-percentile frame-rate floor predicted experience better than average fps, and quality fell linearly with frame-time variance.
  • NVIDIA found 12 ms of frame-time variation hurt perceived smoothness, while 4 ms barely registered.

Every platform converges on the same answer:

  • Apple calls a frame that arrives late a hitch. It treats a hitch-time ratio under 5 ms/s as "mostly unnoticeable" and over 10 ms/s as critical. It advises dropping to a steady 30 rather than a jittery 40–60.
  • Android's frame pacing library was built because a "30 fps" game showing frames of 49, 16, 33 ms is perceived as stutter.
  • Digital Foundry faulted Bloodborne for averaging 30 fps with 16–66 ms frame times.
  • Pebble ran its whole animation system on a self-correcting 33 ms timer.
  • LVGL uses a 33 ms default refresh period.

When a frame is late, Apple, Android, Pebble and LVGL all jump ahead: progress is (now − start) / duration. A fixed step per frame makes the animation slow down, and its timing goes rubbery. Huge stalls are clamped so nothing teleports.

The steady 30 lane is smoother than either lane with late frames, even though the late frames are rare.
  • Grid: nos_main.c paces frames with an absolute esp_timer deadline (next += period). After more than one period late it realigns instead of bursting.
  • Time steps: nos_frame_run quantises elapsed time to whole periods k ∈ [1,4]. Animation jumps ahead after a late frame and clamps at 133 ms. This matches the industry consensus.
  • First frame: transitions start one step in (T.tw.t0 -= nos_dt), so input moves the very next frame.
  • Measured: the board suite showed 0 late frames in every bench.
  • Hitch metric: add hitch ms/s (missed-slot time per second of motion) and a longest-miss-run count to NOSPERF, so cadence regressions show up as one number.
  • Expensive first frames: make sure the animation clock starts at the first presented frame (Android's commitAnimationFrame rule), so a slow snapshot frame doesn't eat the first 30 ms of a transition.

03The per-frame step budget

R2. Anything the eye tracks moves ≤ 16 px per frame; 5–10 px is the safe zone.
  • Short programmatic transitions may reach ~64 px per frame (about 1° of visual angle).
  • Bigger jumps happen only as a deliberate single-frame cut (§5), never as a mid-speed frame of a smooth curve.
  • Thin text strokes step visibly at 1–2 px per frame, so move text slowly or fade it.
  • Dot fields move by less than half a dot pitch per frame, or in whole pitches.

Apparent motion needs each step to land within the visual system's short-range window: about 15 arcmin (Braddick's dmax), which is ~17 px on this watch at 30 cm.

  • Inside the window: motion reads as continuous.
  • Past it: the eye sees an object hopping, and later separate copies.
  • Smooth sampling depends on detail. Burr, Ross and Morrone found sampled motion looks smooth when each step is about ⅛–⅓ of the moving pattern's spatial period:
    • A soft 100 px blob tolerates 13–36 px per frame.
    • Text with a 4–6 px stroke period tolerates 1–2 px.
  • Other converging estimates: the film pan-speed rule, Watson's window of visibility, and Mackin's "50 % of the critical frame rate" all land at roughly 6–17 px per frame at 30 fps.
  • Hold blur: AMOLED holds each frame for the full 33 ms with sub-millisecond pixel response, so a tracking eye sees the per-frame step as a crisp stutter rather than a soft blur.
  • No strobing at 30: black-frame insertion or short persistence doesn't help here. A 30 Hz strobe flickers, and 30 fps content on a 60 Hz strobe double-images.
  • Periodic patterns such as a dot grid alias once they step more than half their period, the wagon-wheel effect.
  • Brightness and contrast make judder worse. Moving layers that are dimmer, like the outgoing screen of a push, judder less.
speed

Three objects at the same speed: a dot, a dot-matrix numeral and a card. Watch the numeral. Its fine dot pattern breaks first, which is why dot-matrix content wants the tightest budget.

Reference speeds: a flick-driven list at release ≈ 1500–3000 px/s; a 466 px push over 340 ms averages 1370 px/s and peaks far higher.

Slowing down is often not an option: a whole-screen push at ≤ 16 px/frame would take a full second. That is why the rest of this document is about which frames to show, not how to show more of them.

04Author spacing for frames, not milliseconds

R3. Treat every animation as a short list of frames:
  • Choose the curve by its worst frame, not its shape on a graph. The biggest step should be ≤ 1.6× the average step.
  • Frame 1 shows a meaningful part of the move: at least 15 %, never ~0.
  • The last moving frame still moves ≥ 1 px.
  • Durations are whole frames: micro moves 3–6 frames, transitions 7–12.

A cubic-bezier that looks gentle as a curve can hide a brutal frame at 30 fps.

  • EASE_DECEL (Material's "emphasized decelerate") starts at about 14× linear speed. Over 250 ms it puts 68 % of a 466 px move into the first frame.
  • EASE_STANDARD peaks at 4× linear around t ≈ 0.12. That lands in frame 1 or 2, so it jumps 130–165 px there.
  • Ease-in curves waste frame 1: a 3 % first step on a 60 px move is a 2 px "did it start?" frame.

Proposal: EASE_STEADY = cubic-bezier(0.61, 1, 0.88, 1), a close fit to ease-out sine. It is for arrivals that must still feel instant:

  • Frame 1 already moves (~16 % of the travel).
  • Speed falls evenly.
  • The peak step is 1.6× the average, against 2.8–3.7× for EASE_STANDARD.
  • It lands with a ~1 % final step instead of a creep.

For moves of only a few frames, write the spacing as a table. Animators' "thirds", the craft research and Pebble all do this:

  • 3 frames: 60 / 90 / 100 %
  • 4 frames: 45 / 78 / 95 / 100 %
  • 6 frames: 28 / 53 / 73 / 87 / 96 / 100 %

Round the cumulative position, never each step. Rounding steps produces lumps such as 9-8-5-5-2-1, and dead frames.

05Moving far: fade it or cut it

R4. A programmatic change that covers the screen should not slide the screen. Do one of two things:
  • (a) Move less, fade more: ≤ 60 px of travel, with an ink fade or a reveal carrying the change.
  • (b) Cut: a 1-frame anticipation, the bulk of the move in one frame (optionally smeared), then a 4-2-1-0 px settle. No frame sits at a mid-speed position for the eye to track.
Finger-driven moves stay 1:1, because the finger is the motion.

Motion should be either slow or brief. Smooth pursuit (the eye locking onto a moving target) starts about 100 ms after motion begins. A move that completes within about 5 frames is over before the eye can follow it, so it reads as one decisive change rather than as judder. Long, medium-fast motion is the worst of both worlds: the eye tracks it, and every step is too big.

Two industry answers fit a 30 fps budget:

  • Material's shared-axis transition travels only 30 dp (~61 px here) over 300 ms and lets a fade-through do the rest. The outgoing content fades over the first 35 %, the incoming over the rest.
  • Pebble's "moook" curve was built for its 30 fps round screen: a 1 px nudge, a 20 px lead-in, one hard cut to 4 px past the target, then 2, 1, 0. Seven frames, 231 ms, and none of them at a speed where judder shows.

Fading is cheap on true black. A screen fades by drawing its primitives in scaled ink (NOS's nos_ink_dim), so there's no per-pixel blend, and a sequential fade-through never composites two screens at once.

Closing at constant speed, which is what NOS does now, is acceptable because the steps are even (466 px over 6.6 frames ≈ 70 px/frame). Shortening the close travel would make it better.

06Crisp endings

R5. End every motion within 1 px (or 1 dot) of the target by snapping. No spring or tween may spend more than 2 frames moving less than 1.5 px per frame.

Critically damped springs approach their target exponentially, and NOS settles them within min(0.25, 1 % of the move). That's precise but costly:

  • A 466 px SPRING_PAGE move spends 6 of its 19 frames creeping under 1.5 px.
  • The creep is invisible as motion, but every one of those frames is a repaint, and during transitions a full one.
  • It also smears the "click" of the arrival.

Android Compose ends spring animations at a 1 px visibility threshold for exactly this reason.

07Honest sub-pixel motion

R6.
  • Anti-aliased primitives (circles, pills, rings, dots) take float positions: pass the unrounded value.
  • Integer-positioned content (text, sprites, rects) rounds the cumulative position once per frame. Never accumulate rounded steps.
  • Slow continuous motion of fine detail uses whole-pixel speeds (1, 2 or 3 px/frame) or moves dots by brightness steps.
  • Dot grids move in whole dot pitches or under half a pitch.

NOS's gfx_fill_circle, gfx_fill_pill and gfx_ring take floats, so they can be positioned at sub-pixel accuracy for free. Text, sprites and gfx_fill_rect are integer.

A list sliding at 1.33 px/frame steps 1, 1, 2, 1, 1, 2…, which shows as shimmer on thin strokes. The cinema "7-second rule" for pans works out to about 2.2 px/frame here, which is why slow scrolls of fine detail judder unless their speed is a whole number of pixels.

LED-sign practice offers a cheap middle ground: move a dot one pixel by splitting its brightness between the old and new positions (4:0, 3:1, 2:2, 1:3).

08Smear what is fast

R7. Small objects that must move faster than ~16 px/frame (seconds-hand sweeps, spinners, dot bursts, the jump frame of a cut) get a cheap smear: one ghost at the previous frame's position at ~45 % ink plus an optional second at ~20 %, or a capsule stretched along the velocity. Ghosts last ≤ 2 frames and are never fainter than ~20 % ink.

Hand animators fake motion blur with smear frames, multiples and speed lines, because real blur muddies the in-betweens. Games do it with afterimages (Celeste, Mega Man X).

Film's 180° shutter sets the right amount: a blur of about half the per-frame step. Blur helps motion the eye is not tracking, such as flings, snaps and the jump frame of a cut. On a finger-tracked drag it only adds to the hold smear, so never smear drags.

On this watch a ghost is one extra primitive drawn in dimmed ink: cost class "cheap". There's no per-pixel pass. AMOLED adds no response blur of its own, so the smear has to be drawn.

Keep ghosts short and not too faint. Users report very dim near-black content smearing purple on AMOLED.

angular speed treatment
A dot sweeping round the bezel. At 360°/s it moves 63 px per frame. With no treatment the eye sees separate dots; with one ghost it reads as a single fast object. The dot-ring trail lights the bezel dots it passes and lets them decay. That fits NOS's dot language and costs a few circles.

09Quantise on purpose

R8. Where a change is discrete (a digit, a state, a level), animate it as discrete: dot morphs, split-flap steps, whole-row odometer rolls, or ring dots filling one by one. Decorative loops may run on twos (15 fps) as a deliberate style. Never step anything the eye tracks: scrolls, drags or pans.

Low frame rates read as style when the stepping is a choice with a contrast. Spider-Verse animates Miles on twos and Peter on ones. Anime holds poses and skips in-betweens to exaggerate speed. Split-flap boards and Nothing's Glyph Matrix make the discrete state change be the motion.

A dot-matrix numeral that slides is a fine pattern moving fast, the worst case from R2. The same numeral morphing its dots in place has no spatial step at all.

10Whole-frame rhythm

R9. Staggers are whole frames (1 or 2 per item), start at frame 0, and cover ≤ 8 items and ≤ ~10 frames in total. Fades use NOS's 16 ink levels, spaced by perceived brightness rather than linearly. Durations come from the frame table: 3 (snap), 4–6 (micro), 7–9 (component), 10–12 (screen).

A 20 ms stagger at 30 fps starts items on frames 0, 0, 1, 1, 2, 3, 3, 4: uneven clumps. A 1-frame stagger is a perfect cascade.

Fades read evenly when their levels follow perceived brightness (for example 100 / 70 / 45 / 25 / 10 / 0 %). Linear alpha spends half its steps where the eye can barely tell them apart.

11Touch latency

R10.
  • Sample touch at the controller's rate (60–100 Hz) with timestamps, and feed every sample to the velocity tracker.
  • Latch the newest sample immediately before tick.
  • Extrapolate drag positions to the present time: about ½–¾ of a frame (16–25 ms), at most half the last delta, and never past a release.
  • Taps show a visible response within 1–2 frames.
  • Fling velocity is a least-squares fit over the last 100 ms (≥ 3 samples), zero if the finger has been still for 40 ms.

On direct touch, people notice drag latency from about 6–11 ms, improvements of 8 ms are detectable, and dragging performance degrades above about 25 ms. Taps are more forgiving, with noticeable latency starting around 64–69 ms. At 30 fps the drag pipeline is long:

  1. The touch is read at the start of frame n.
  2. It is ticked and rasterised.
  3. It is flushed over ~23 ms.
  4. The panel scans it out on its next pass.

Content therefore shows a finger position that is roughly 1–2 frames old: 50–60 ms end to end. A drag at 1000 px/s trails the finger by ~60 px (4.5 mm). Drags at 30 fps will always feel a little sticky. The fix is to cut latency first and predict the rest; in one study, predicting about 25 ms ahead restored pointing performance.

Android resamples touch to (frame time − 5 ms) and predicts at most 8 ms, capped at half the last delta. Chrome found that adding half a frame of prediction minimised scroll lag. Inconsistent sample counts per frame (2 vs 1) were its biggest source of jank.

pipeline latency prediction
Drag inside the watch. The blue ring is your pointer and the card is what a 30 fps pipeline shows. Turn on prediction: the card catches up on steady drags and overshoots a little on sudden stops. That's why prediction is capped and dropped at release. Your browser adds its own latency on top.
  • Velocity: velocity() in nos.c is already a least-squares fit over 100 ms.
  • Touch rate: on the watch, nos_main.c reads the FT3168 once per frame (30 Hz) at the top of the loop. The fit therefore sees only about 3 samples, while the simulator delivers 60 Hz (6 samples). Drags carry up to a frame of extra latency, and nothing is predicted.

12Tearing without TE

R11. Design motion that hides tears:
  • Prefer vertical travel and top-to-bottom reveals.
  • Keep horizontal per-frame deltas small, or make them a single-frame cut so a tear lasts one frame.
  • Prefer fades and in-place changes for full-screen transitions.
  • Keep writes in row order.
Judge tearing on the real watch, never by bench numbers.

The CO5300 has a single GRAM that it scans at ~60 Hz ± 2 %, and we write it at 30 Hz over 23.5 ms with no sync.

  • Where the tear comes from: the panel re-reads GRAM at its own speed, so the slow write doesn't shear the image. Wherever the scan overtakes the writer, rows above show one frame and rows below another. The seam is a step of one frame's motion.
  • Why it moves: the oscillator's tolerance makes the seam drift. At a 1 % mismatch it cycles every ~1.7 s, and it shifts with temperature.
  • Seam by motion direction:
    • Horizontal motion breaks vertical edges sideways, the most visible case.
    • Vertical motion duplicates or cuts a strip.
    • Fades show as a faint brightness seam.

Paced RGB332 writes almost never cross the scan. That's a model prediction to verify on the watch.

The repo's own history agrees:

  • Column-band flushes benchmarked faster, but tore visibly worse. Row stripes stay the default.
  • Scan-line readback (45h) is impossible on this board.
  • Locking our cadence to exactly half the panel rate would only freeze a random tear position, since we know neither the panel's exact period (±2 %) nor its phase. A still seam is less distracting than a rolling one, but only a slow-motion camera calibration could get us there.

The one engine experiment worth running is a write speed matched to the scan (RGB332 paced to ~16.7 ms per full height), and it needs your eyes on the watch.

13Stable frame cost

R12. Motion frames stay under ~80 % of the budget (≤ 27 ms work), so one busy frame doesn't break the cadence. Everything an animation touches is baked and allocated before it starts. Moving regions stay small, and damage is merged in row order.

NOS already learned most of this on the board:

  • libm calls cost ~1000 cycles.
  • A per-pixel dim pass costs 17 ms.
  • First-use bakes made 50–500 ms frames.
  • Screens draw once per 16-row stripe, so per-item work is multiplied ~30×.

Industry agrees. TouchGFX says invalidate less and replace computed graphics with images. LVGL users saw 40 fps fall to 4 by re-decoding per frame. PSRAM reads run at ~57 MB/s, against ~366 MB/s for internal RAM.

The new rules in this document mostly lower cost: shorter travel means smaller damage, and crisp endings remove creep repaints. The ghost and fade techniques use dimmed ink, not blending.

16How we measure

  • Motion audit (new, simulator). Every tween and spring that drives a position reports its pixel span. The sim logs each frame's step per animated value and flags anything over the class budget, frames below 1.5 px (creep), and first frames below 15 %. Run it over the full tour and every app's motion scripts.
  • Cost model and damage check. These are unchanged: p95 < 30 ms in motion, 0 late frames idle, and --damagecheck byte-identical.
  • Board suite. gestures.py, survey.py, facecost2.py, slides.py and bootjank.py must stay at 0 late frames. NOSPERF adds hitch ms/s.
  • Eyes on glass. Tearing and "does this feel smooth" are judged on the watch, never by numbers alone.
